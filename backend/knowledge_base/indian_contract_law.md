# Indian Contract Law Knowledge Base

## Overview

Indian contracts are primarily governed by the Indian Contract Act, 1872. The Act establishes the rules relating to formation, performance, breach, and enforceability of contracts in India.

A contract is an agreement enforceable by law. Every contract is an agreement, but not every agreement becomes a contract. To be legally enforceable, an agreement must satisfy the requirements prescribed under Indian law.

---

## Essential Elements of a Valid Contract

A valid contract generally requires:

1. Offer by one party.
2. Acceptance by another party.
3. Lawful consideration.
4. Free consent of all parties.
5. Competency of parties to contract.
6. Lawful object and purpose.
7. Intention to create legal obligations.
8. Certainty of terms.
9. Possibility of performance.

Failure to satisfy these requirements may result in the agreement being void, voidable, or unenforceable.

---

## Offer and Acceptance

An offer is a proposal made by one party expressing willingness to enter into a contract on specified terms.

Acceptance occurs when the person receiving the offer agrees to those terms without material modification.

A valid acceptance must:

* Be absolute and unconditional.
* Correspond exactly with the offer.
* Be communicated to the offeror.
* Occur before the offer is withdrawn or expires.

A counter-offer generally rejects the original offer and creates a new proposal.

---

## Consideration

Consideration refers to something of value exchanged between parties.

Consideration may consist of:

* Payment of money.
* Provision of services.
* Transfer of property.
* Promise to perform or refrain from an act.

Agreements without lawful consideration are generally unenforceable unless they fall within recognized legal exceptions.

---

## Free Consent

Consent is considered free when it is not caused by:

* Coercion.
* Undue influence.
* Fraud.
* Misrepresentation.
* Mistake.

If consent is obtained through improper means, the affected party may have the right to avoid the contract.

---

## Competency to Contract

A person is generally competent to contract if they:

* Have attained the age of majority.
* Are of sound mind.
* Are not disqualified by law.

Agreements entered into by minors are generally void.

---

## Lawful Object

The purpose of the agreement must be lawful.

An agreement may be invalid if its object:

* Is forbidden by law.
* Defeats the provisions of law.
* Is fraudulent.
* Causes injury to persons or property.
* Is considered immoral or opposed to public policy.

---

## Void Agreements

Void agreements are agreements that cannot be enforced by law.

Examples may include:

* Agreements with unlawful objects.
* Agreements lacking consideration.
* Agreements with uncertain terms.
* Agreements involving impossible acts.

---

## Performance of Contracts

Parties are generally required to perform their contractual obligations according to agreed terms.

Performance may occur through:

* Actual performance.
* Tender of performance.
* Mutual agreement.
* Operation of law.

Failure to perform may constitute breach.

---

## Breach of Contract

A breach occurs when a party fails to perform a contractual obligation without lawful excuse.

Types of breach include:

* Actual breach.
* Anticipatory breach.

A breach may entitle the affected party to seek legal remedies.

---

## Remedies for Breach

Common remedies include:

### Damages

Monetary compensation intended to place the injured party in the position they would have occupied had the contract been performed.

### Specific Performance

A court order directing a party to perform contractual obligations where monetary damages are inadequate.

### Injunction

A court order restraining a party from performing certain acts.

### Rescission

Cancellation of the contract and restoration of parties to their original positions where appropriate.

---

## Indemnity

An indemnity is a promise by one party to compensate another for specified losses.

Indemnity clauses are commonly used to allocate contractual risk.

When reviewing contracts, broad or unlimited indemnity obligations should be treated as high-risk provisions.

---

## Guarantee

A guarantee involves:

* Principal debtor.
* Creditor.
* Surety.

The surety agrees to perform obligations or satisfy liabilities if the principal debtor fails to do so.

---

## Agency

An agency relationship exists when one person is authorized to act on behalf of another.

Actions performed by an authorized agent may legally bind the principal.

---

## Limitation of Liability

Parties frequently include clauses limiting liability.

Common limitations include:

* Liability caps.
* Exclusion of indirect damages.
* Exclusion of consequential damages.

Contracts containing unlimited liability obligations should be flagged for review.

---

## Dispute Resolution

Contracts commonly provide mechanisms for resolving disputes, including:

* Negotiation.
* Mediation.
* Arbitration.
* Litigation.

Important considerations include:

* Governing law.
* Jurisdiction.
* Seat of arbitration.
* Language of proceedings.

---

## Contract Review Risk Indicators

High-risk indicators include:

* Unlimited indemnity.
* Unlimited liability.
* Unclear payment terms.
* Unilateral termination rights.
* Broad intellectual property assignment.
* Perpetual confidentiality obligations.
* Mandatory foreign jurisdiction for small-value disputes.
* Ambiguous scope of work.
* Missing acceptance criteria.
* Automatic renewal without notice.

Medium-risk indicators include:

* Vague deliverables.
* Missing timelines.
* Undefined key terms.
* Broad discretionary powers.

Low-risk indicators include:

* Formatting inconsistencies.
* Minor drafting ambiguities.
